Working Papers

Job Market Paper

Where Discretion Lives: Product Stakes and the Demographic Gap in Consumer Financial Restitution

Abstract

Demographic gaps in consumer redress are usually modeled as a property of firms or of consumers. I show they are better understood as a property of tasks. Using CFPB data on millions of complaints, I document that the demographic gap in financial restitution is close to zero in low-stakes, easily verified products such as credit cards and deposit accounts, and large in high-stakes, contested products such as mortgages, student loans, and debt collection. The gap appears where firms exercise discretion and disappears where they apply rules. To test whether firms rather than filers generate this pattern, I use confidential CFPB monitoring reports as a plausibly exogenous source of variation, which are issued to firms on a staggered schedule and never disclosed publicly. Following a report, restitution does not rise for the average complaint; instead the return to filer self-advocacy roughly doubles and the return to documentation more than doubles, and relief shifts toward high-income, high-education, Asian, and White filers. Firms therefore respond to supervision by pricing filer-supplied signals more heavily, which widens the gap it might have been expected to close.

Revisiting Staggered Differences-in-Differences: Evidence from State Corporate Tax Rate Effects on Firms' Leverage Decisions

Abstract

I reexamine the staggered treatment adoption of state corporate tax changes on leverage using new econometric techniques. I show that the estimated effect of state corporate tax increases on change in long-term book leverage is much smaller than original and overall insignificant and is slightly larger in magnitude for tax decreases but still overall insignificant. Heterogeneous treatment effects in particular groups cause the bias. My results suggest that the true effect of state corporate tax increases on leverage is not as large and significant, and other papers should be cautious of unusually large and significant effects in similar settings.

Corporate Tax Rate Effects on Firms' Capital Structure Decisions: Evidence from the Trump Tax Reform

Abstract

This paper studies how corporate tax reductions and interest expense limits affect firms' capital structure decisions. Using two important provisions from the 2017 Trump tax reform as identification and firm-year level data, I show that firms decrease their book leverage by 3.3% of total assets on average. This effect is mainly driven by a reduction in debt issuance and high-interest expense firms. My results suggest that large reductions in corporate tax rates combined with interest expense deduction limits have significant impacts on firms' leverage most likely due to a multiplier effect of reduction in tax shields.
